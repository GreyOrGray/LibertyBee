This is no longer a live project, but it is a finished record.

After 2+ years, I’ve decided to wrap development on the Liberty Bee project. I still think it’s a good idea, but I’ve poured a lot of energy into this and, honestly, I need a break. So, for now, the project is mothballed with its record complete. Every number on these pages is final, every run reproduces from a seed, and the full stack — engine, data, tools — is published for whoever picks it up next. Maybe that’s me, someday. Or maybe it’s you. What was planned next lives on the method page. What follows is the complete answer to the question we set out to test.

Liberty Bee

Can housing be run without extraction — and still survive?

Short answer: our model says yes, it can.

Most landlords keep a few escape hatches: raise the rent, cut the maintenance, skim the surplus. Liberty Bee gives up all of them. We simulated twenty years, across every funding level and market from calm to crash, to find out whether that can actually last — and we're showing you everything, including the parts that don't flatter us, and where every number comes from.

Liberty Bee is a released, finished record. The numbers on these pages are final: the complete measurement, reproducible end to end from the published engine and data. Legacy numbers can be found at previous versions, and a current record of known issues with the model is available at known issues.

What we built

Not a slide deck — a simulation you can re-run.

Liberty Bee is an event-driven Monte-Carlo engine: twenty simulated years, run across funding ladders from $2M to $20M and every market regime, from calm to crash. Over three versions we’ve run 148,000 simulations — just shy of 3 million simulated years — expanding and refining the engine based on the data they generated (that count skips the throwaway development and verification runs along the way). It doesn’t argue the idea works — it stress-tests it, and reports what happened, including the runs that died. Every number below is one of those outputs.9 How it’s built and how to reproduce it is the method page.

Before the numbers — one place, on purpose

Everything here started with one real city: Salem, Massachusetts.

The bulk of the data here — every market fact, every simulated year, every survival number on this site — is grounded in one real place, not a made-up "Anytown" tuned to flatter the model. Salem is the case study; the squeeze it measures — rents outrunning pay, almost nothing open, buying in taking real capital — is not unique to it. If your city feels like that too, the shape of these findings translates. The exact numbers are Salem's.

If you find yourself saying "I don't care about Salem", that's okay — we did some simulations of other regions as well and you'll find that info below. And you don't have to take the translation on faith: every regional fact in the model is a named input, not a law. The current release can load a region built from your own data — properties, rents, incomes, taxes — and re-run everything against your city. Why Salem · how to point it at your region.

The idea, the simulation, and what it found

What Liberty Bee is — starting with what it isn't.

Before the findings: an honest fence around the claim.

First — what this is not

This is not "affordable housing," and it does not solve poverty.

Liberty Bee is not subsidized or income-restricted housing. The baseline model assumes no tax credit, no affordability covenant, no government operating subsidy — because the whole point is to test whether the model itself can stand without them.

And it does not solve poverty. That's a real problem with real, established answers — vouchers, public housing, direct subsidy — and we can't match them as the model stands. The deeply low-income renter needs help no below-market landlord can provide; pretending otherwise would be dishonest.

What it tries to be is narrower and testable: more affordable than the market, free of the usual extractive incentives — no rent hikes on people who stay, no punitive fees, no surplus skimmed off — and still solvent twenty years on. That's the entire claim. Someday a future version might accept vouchers and stack with subsidy; not this one, and we say so on purpose.

The idea

It was never about making money.

In our original written proposal, the mission was stated simply: provide stable, dignified housing while fostering long-term community resilience.

The idea isn't to make money. It never was. Not much of a sales pitch, we know — but bear with us. The idea is to deliver immediate, meaningful relief against one of the biggest stressors in our society: housing.

The Liberty Bee model isn't about investing in housing — it's about anchoring families, preserving community integrity, and reshaping what affordability looks like. It's a non-extractive, sustainable, community-driven future for housing. It's about investing in the people.

The point was never the balance sheet. It's what people keep.

Solvency is the floor, not the goal. So the model measures the thing that actually matters: how much better off tenants are.

That savings is real money that stayed in the community instead of leaving as someone's profit — and it compounds: the longer you stay, the deeper your discount gets.

the honest catch

In-kind housing help is a famously inefficient transfer — economists find it can cost a provider more than a dollar for each dollar a tenant truly values (the classic estimate is ~2.4×15). We won't pretend otherwise. What our model can measure is the straight transfer — every dollar Liberty Bee gives up in rent is a dollar a tenant keeps. What it can't measure is how much a tenant privately values that housing versus cash — so we don't fake that number, or put one on what a cash check can't buy: stability, not being displaced, roots.

The refusal

Liberty Bee gives up the landlord's playbook.

  • Norent hikes on someone who stays — ever. If anything, your rent falls the longer you're here.
  • Nocutting the maintenance to pad the margin — buildings are kept up, not run into the ground.
  • Nosurplus skimmed off as profit — it's reinvested in tenants, buildings, and reserve.

Instead

Liberty Bee rewards tenants from day one.

  • YesBelow-market rent — every lease signs under the market rate.
  • YesRegular rent reductions over time — the longer you stay, the deeper it gets.
  • YesA redeemable credit system to offset rent further.

Everything is machinery in service of one idea: the value tenants and a community create should stay with them.

The exact numbers behind each — the signing discount, the reduction schedule, the credit — are below, in the findings.

Tenant credits

A hidden paycheck that follows you.

Beyond the rent discount, Liberty Bee banks 10% of every on-time rent payment into a Tenant Credit that belongs to the household. It's not cash, not a loan, not equity, and it can't be sold or transferred away.

How and when you use it: once a year you can draw up to a month's rent from your balance — either as a routine credit toward a month you choose, or as hardship relief when a month goes sideways. When you move out in good standing, whatever's left can go toward your final month's rent, and it stays redeemable for two years after you leave (an eviction forfeits it). It only ever builds on rent paid on time, and it never touches damages — that's what a deposit is for.

Finding — the bottom line

How much stays with the tenants.

Put it together — below-market rent plus credits actually redeemed — and you can watch the total value kept in the community instead of extracted as profit, piling up over twenty years. More starting capital doesn't just serve more people; it keeps more money local, and the gap widens every year.

An org funded at the $7M floor leaves about $3.9M with its tenants over twenty years; at $20M, about $11.1M. None of it leaves as someone’s return — and that’s only the tenant side: the wages paid to local staff and the money spent maintaining the buildings stay in the community on top of it.

Finding — why people stay

Treat people well, and they stay. Now we can show the mechanism.

The whole theory of change is a loop: a below-market deal that deepens with tenure means the longer you stay, the more you'd give up by leaving — so people put down roots, and stability compounds. This isn't just hope — below-market tenants are measurably about 19% less likely to move13. The model captures it directly: your odds of leaving fall as your deal gets better.

not all loyalty is love

We're honest about why people stay — and it shifts over time. Early in a tenancy, "nowhere else affordable to go" is a big part of it: the discount is still shallow and the market is tight. But as your below-market deal deepens with tenure, the deal itself becomes the reason. Averaged across a whole tenancy, that "nowhere to go" constraint accounts for about 48% of why people stay — a large minority, but the deal itself is still the bigger half.14

But let's be real: "nowhere to go" is only a win for a traditional landlord. Our goal isn't captive tenants. Yes, it keeps people in the Liberty Bee system — but that's not why we want them here. It's a symptom of a broken housing landscape, and even where it "benefits" us on paper, we'd far rather people stay because they want to than because they have nowhere else to go.

How is this even possible?

No mortgage. That's the whole trick.

Liberty Bee buys its buildings outright — no debt, no lender, no interest payment that has to be met before anything else.

That one fact turns a nice idea into a workable one. A leveraged landlord has to maximize rent and cut corners — the bank doesn't care about your tenants. With no debt, the money that would've gone to a mortgage cycles back into the reserve, the next building, and the people living there. It's also why Liberty Bee can absorb the cost of its own kindness — flat rents, deep discounts, keeping people housed — and just grow slower, where a leveraged operator would default.

the short version

No mortgage, so the money stays in the mission.

Finding — does it survive?

From $7M, every run survives.

At fair regional wages and the current deal, this is Salem's answer: 500 runs per funding level, twenty simulated years each, every market regime. Below $7M the odds fall away fast — 99% at $6.5M, 94% at $6M, a coin flip near $5M. From $7M up, every single run survives, all the way to $20M. No wobble, no high-capital trap — a clean floor.

the runway

Thin funding doesn't fail loudly up front — it bleeds out. At $5M, 99% are still standing at year five and 71% at year ten — but only 44.2% finish the twenty (the median failure at that level comes in month 119, year 10). At $6M the bleed slows to 6 runs in 100; at $7M it stops: no run dies, at any point, ever.

Finding — reach

Past the floor, money doesn't buy safety. It buys reach.

Every funding level from $7M up survives every run — so what does more capital do? It houses more people. The median organization shelters 94 households over its twenty years at $7M, and 291 at $20M — roughly 17 more households per additional million, at the same certainty.

Beyond Salem

What about areas outside of Salem?

Same engine, same deal, eight more regions — each priced from its own public data and paying its own metro’s wages. The answer to “how much money does this take?” is: it depends where.

What the geography says

The North Shore compresses. High-wage metros stretch.

Under the current deal, six of the seven Massachusetts towns land inside a $1.5M window ($6.5M–$8M) — town choice matters less than you’d think once the deal and wages are held honest. The stretch comes from labor markets: San Francisco needs $10M, and Tacoma — priced at Seattle-metro wages — runs 97–99% from about $7M but never fully cleans below $18M. In high-wage metros the answer is a probability band, not a threshold.

We also looked at Santa Fe, Denton, and New Orleans — and we couldn’t identify enough public data to populate our set for them honestly, so they aren’t shown. Someone with other data access — even MLS access — could run their own: the model takes any region bundle built from your own data, and the wage and deal knobs are all yours to turn.

Blueprint, not a building

What would it take to make this real? Capital. Full stop.

There is no funded Liberty Bee. No buildings, no tenants, no bank account. Right now it's a model — a rigorous, reproducible argument that the idea holds up.

Most housing pitches ask you to trust a vision and write a check. We'd rather prove the thing survives twenty years of simulated pressure first, in the open, before anyone risks a dollar — or a home — on it. Having done that, the honest answer to "so what's missing?" is one word: deep pockets.

The model runs on buying buildings outright — no debt — which takes real money up front, and we have exactly none. But it already shows how much it takes (about $7M to clear the survival threshold at fair regional wages) and that it holds. So this isn't a plea for faith — it's a number, a method, and a result anyone can check, and then capital. Nothing else stands between the model and a real operation.
